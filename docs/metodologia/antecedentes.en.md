# Introduction and Background

## Road infrastructure and risk in Latin America

Transport infrastructure is the backbone of economic and social development in Latin America and the Caribbean. It facilitates trade, connectivity, and access to essential services. However, the region faces high exposure to socio-natural disasters — hurricanes, tropical storms, earthquakes, landslides, and floods — that not only cause loss of life and economic damage, but also severely disrupt road networks, isolate communities, and hinder emergency response.

Improving the resilience of road infrastructure is not optional: it is an urgent necessity to protect lives, safeguard investments, and ensure the continuity of sustainable development in the face of a changing climate. In this context, having methodologies and tools that enable proactive risk assessment and management becomes fundamental for the countries of the region.

## The original Blue Spot Analysis (BSA 1.0)

The approach known as **Blue Spot Analysis** was originally developed by the **Danish Road Directorate (DRD)**, through its technical center Danish Road Institute (DRI), within the framework of the **SWAMP** project (*Storm WAter prevention – Methods to Predict damage from the water stream in and near road pavements in lowland areas*), carried out in the early 2000s.

Its core objective was to systematically identify road segments with the highest probability of flooding and significant consequences under current and future intense rainfall scenarios. The methodology was structured in three hierarchical levels:

1. **Identification of depressions** in digital elevation models (DEM).
2. **Sensitivity analysis** to extreme rainfall through GIS simulations.
3. **Detailed hydrodynamic modeling** (1D/2D) to estimate depth and dynamics of surface runoff at critical points.

This modular architecture allows for a progressive reduction of the analysis universe — from the entire national road network to priority segments — and integrates climate change adaptation criteria. The model was adopted by road agencies in Sweden and the Netherlands, establishing itself as a reference practice for resilient road planning in Europe (Danish Road Directorate, 2005; European Environment Agency, 2013).

## Adoption and expansion by the IDB

Building on this methodology, the **Inter-American Development Bank (IDB)** developed a complementary approach that extends the conceptual application of the original BSA. The IDB incorporated not only flood risk, but also hydrometeorological and geophysical hazards, orienting the tool toward **decision-making on investment prioritization** in road infrastructure from an economic and functional perspective.

The first application of BSA in Latin America was carried out in the **Dominican Republic**, laying the groundwork for the updated version. From that experience, opportunities for improvement emerged in terms of scalability, ease of data access, and participation of users external to the IDB.

## Toward BSA 2.0: a new methodological paradigm

**BSA 2.0** was born as a direct response to these improvement opportunities and the need identified in other regions to replicate the analysis in their portfolios in a dynamic and participatory manner. Its development is led by the **Transport Division (INE/TSP)** of the IDB, with the support of:

- The **Climate Change Division (CSD/CCS)**, which promotes climate resilience as a cross-cutting component.
- The **Disaster Risk Management and Climate Adaptation Division (CSD/DRM)**, which contributed the conceptualization of the multi-hazard risk module.

### Conceptual evolution of risk assessment

BSA 2.0 is part of a broader evolution in the field of disaster risk analysis:

| Period | Conceptual milestone |
|---|---|
| ~1700 | First collective insurance systems (Great Fire of London, 1666) |
| 1960s | U.S. Army Corps of Engineers flood models (USACE) and Cornell's probabilistic seismic risk analysis (1968) |
| 1974 | Gilbert White integrates human decisions and natural phenomena into risk analysis |
| 1980s | Damage functions and loss curves in flood studies (UNDRO, Parker, Smith & Ward) |
| 1992 | Hurricane Andrew drives private catastrophe modeling (RMS, AIR Worldwide) |
| 2004 | Merz and Thieken systematize probabilistic flood risk analysis with three components: hazard, exposure, vulnerability |
| 2015 | The Global Assessment Report (UNISDR) applies the probabilistic approach to more than 140 countries |
| Present | AI and machine learning for hazard prediction and automatic exposure identification |

BSA 2.0 draws on the framework of **Merz and Thieken (2004)** and adapts it to the operational contexts of the region, adopting a simplified probabilistic model that estimates the Expected Annual Loss (EAL) through interpolation between discrete hazard levels — the most commonly available data format in LAC countries — rather than exhaustive stochastically simulated event catalogs.

### Alignment with IDB institutional frameworks

The design of BSA 2.0 is consistent with the **IDB Disaster and Climate Change Risk Assessment Methodology (2019)**, which proposes a gradual approach in five sequential steps. BSA 2.0 can modulate its application from a large-scale exploratory assessment (step 1) to a detailed estimation with intervention measures (steps 4 and 5), adjusting the technical scope to the institutional context and available resources.

## Current challenges and future outlook

Among the main recognized challenges for BSA 2.0 are:

- **Data availability**: road inventories, hazard models, and vulnerability functions vary in quality and coverage between countries.
- **Institutional capacity**: sustained operation of the platform requires technical teams trained in GIS, hydrology, and risk management.
- **Scalability and participation**: the platform must allow national users to update input data and run their own analyses.

The growing incorporation of artificial intelligence — for hazard prediction, automatic exposure identification, and decision optimization — opens the possibility of evolving BSA 2.0 toward hybrid schemes that combine classical probabilistic models with AI components, expanding its predictive capacity and applicability in high-uncertainty environments (Camps-Valls et al., 2024).
